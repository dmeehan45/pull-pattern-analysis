# Falsification protocol

## Purpose

Selection bias and confirmation bias are the default failure modes of qualitative demand analysis.

If a team starts with enthusiastic interviews, closed-won customers, or hand-picked anecdotes and asks "what pattern do we see?", an agent can produce an articulate but unsupported story.

The agent therefore has a second job beyond synthesis: **try to make candidate patterns fail**.

## The six attacks

Run all six against every material candidate pattern.

### 1. Negative-case attack

Find cases that share the supposed Project but do not show the predicted PULL.

Ask:
- What is different?
- Is the forcing condition actually the differentiator?
- Are options better?
- Is commitment absent?
- Is the buyer different?

A pattern becomes stronger by explaining both presence and absence.

### 2. Selection attack

Reconstruct how the corpus came to exist.

Ask:
- Were only willing interviewees included?
- Only customers?
- Only late-stage prospects?
- Only one acquisition channel?
- Only people who responded to solution-led outreach?
- Were failed or boring conversations retained?

If inclusion is conditioned on signs of demand, frequency claims from the sample are unreliable.

### 3. Independence attack

Look for correlated evidence masquerading as replication.

Examples:
- several people from one organization;
- multiple conversations about one purchase;
- referrals from one champion;
- several companies responding to the same temporary external event.

Reduce the effective case count accordingly.

### 4. Rival-explanation attack

Generate a different causal story that explains the same evidence.

Then identify an observation on which the two stories make different predictions.

Do not ask which story sounds better. Seek discriminating evidence.

### 5. Transaction attack

Follow the pattern all the way to resource allocation.

Ask:
- Who can say yes?
- What gets displaced?
- What approval occurs?
- What procurement/security constraints appear?
- What happens when real switching and implementation costs are introduced?

A pattern that survives user enthusiasm but dies at transaction mechanics may describe need without viable demand.

### 6. Time attack

Ask whether the pattern depends on a temporary state.

Test:
- before vs after the forcing event;
- current vs prior budget period;
- current options vs newly available options;
- behavior after the deadline passes.

Demand hypotheses should expire or be revalidated when their causal conditions change.

## Pattern survival gate

Before promotion to a demand hypothesis, the agent should be able to answer:

- What is the strongest counterexample?
- What is the strongest rival explanation?
- What selection mechanism threatens the inference?
- How many genuinely independent episodes support it?
- What evidence contradicts it?
- What next observation could still kill it?

If these cannot be answered, the pattern is not ready.

## No automatic promotion

There is no minimum anecdote count that automatically makes a pattern "real."

Five highly dependent anecdotes can be weaker than two sharply contrasting independent cases.

The question is not only "how much evidence?" but:

> How hard have we tried to explain this evidence another way?
